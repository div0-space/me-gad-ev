A handoff is more than a plan

2026-10-30 · Maciej + agents · 3 min read · S01E04 · Updated 2026-10-30

On 29 October we worked with agents on a small service that turns a record of completed work into a readable summary. We did not start with an empty directory. Code, architectural decisions and a proposed direction already existed. We needed to establish which parts formed a binding baseline and which were still intentions. We are publishing this account a day later, with room to distinguish the work from its announcement.

A shared foundation before the split

The service had two readers: a person opening a page and an agent fetching data. These were not two independent products. Both needed the same account of the work, presented differently. Splitting the implementation could not mean separately deciding what had happened.

That makes the shared data contract central. If one part exposes a job’s latest state, another should not independently reconstruct its entire history. Even a simple word such as “failure” needs a decision: does it mean a final failure, or also an earlier failure followed by a successful finish? A well-designed table cannot settle that distinction for us.

Parallel does not mean independent

The retained worker records show a specific point of friction. Some tests were written before every required component was available in the main code baseline. A missing service response did not yet prove that the test was wrong. Nor was it a reason to call the whole thing finished. The dependency had to be named and revisited after integration.

This changes how we read an agent’s report. “Ready on my branch” describes a delivered piece. “Works when assembled” is a different statement, requiring its own evidence. We have kept both stages in the account rather than smoothing the history into one trouble-free run.

In the code we returned to while writing, both ways of reading the result use one reader. The tests also compare the order of data between the page and the machine-readable response. That matters more than the mere existence of two working addresses: a reader and an agent should not receive conflicting accounts of the same night.

What the next person receives

Maciej sets the direction and decides what may be published. Agents prepare code, identify dependencies and retain records of their checks. A useful handoff is not measured by the length of its description. It lets the next person establish where they stand and what they own.

Yesterday’s work leaves us with something more useful than a recipe for launching many agents: a distinction between a plan, a delivery and an integrated result. We want to preserve that care in the next task. The next article describes exactly that kind of beginning — before there is a result we can honestly show.
